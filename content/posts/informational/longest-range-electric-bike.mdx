You've seen the number on the product page. "Up to 80 miles on a single charge." Sounds great, until you actually ride the thing and get 35 miles before the battery meter starts blinking red.

Here's the thing. That advertised number isn't a lie, exactly. It's just measured under conditions almost nobody rides in. Flat ground, lowest assist setting, a rider who weighs maybe 150 pounds and never touches the throttle. Your Tuesday commute looks nothing like that.

In my experience testing and researching e-bikes for years, range is the single most misunderstood spec in the entire industry. So let's break down what actually determines how far you'll go, and how to get a number you can actually trust before you buy.

## Why "Advertised Range" and "Real-World Range" Are Rarely the Same

Most manufacturers test range using something close to the SAE J2951 standard or their own in-house version of it. These tests run the bike on flat pavement, at a steady low speed, with pedal assist set to its lowest level, and often with a lighter-than-average test rider.

That's not cheating exactly. It's just optimistic. Brands want a big number on the spec sheet, and testing under ideal conditions gets them one.

The gap between advertised and real range usually falls somewhere between 20 and 50 percent. A bike rated for 60 miles might realistically deliver 35 to 45 miles for an average rider on mixed terrain using moderate assist. That's not a flaw in the bike. It's just physics meeting marketing.

Some brands are more honest than others here. A few now publish a "range estimator" tool that lets you plug in your weight, terrain, and typical assist level, which gives a far more useful number than a single headline figure ever could.

## What Actually Determines Range

Range isn't one variable. It's five or six variables stacked on top of each other, and each one can swing your mileage by a meaningful amount.

**Battery capacity** is the obvious one, measured in watt-hours (Wh). A 500Wh battery holds roughly twice the energy of a 250Wh unit, all else being equal. But capacity alone doesn't tell the whole story, which is why two bikes with identical batteries can have wildly different real-world ranges.

**Rider weight** matters more than most people expect. Moving 220 pounds up a hill takes noticeably more energy than moving 150 pounds on flat ground. Heavier riders should generally expect the lower end of any advertised range, sometimes 15 to 25 percent below what a lighter tester would get.

**Terrain and elevation** are huge. Climbing drains a battery fast because the motor works harder to fight gravity, and you don't get much of that energy back on the way down (regenerative braking on e-bikes is limited at best). A hilly commute can cut your range by a third compared to the same distance on flat roads.

**Assist level** is probably the biggest lever you personally control. Riding on Eco or Level 1 sips power. Cranking it up to Turbo or Level 5 for every hill and stoplight can drain a battery two to three times faster.

**Tire pressure and tire type** play a bigger role than most riders realize. Underinflated tires or knobby off-road tread create more rolling resistance, which means the motor works harder for the same distance. Fat tire bikes in particular tend to see shorter range than their skinny-tire cousins, even with a similar battery.

**Wind and temperature** round out the list. Cold weather can reduce lithium battery output by 10 to 20 percent, and a strong headwind does what you'd expect: makes the motor work harder to maintain speed.

## How to Calculate Realistic Range for Your Riding Style

Want a number you can actually trust before you buy? Start with the advertised range and apply a reality check.

Take the manufacturer's top-line figure and cut it by 30 to 40 percent as a baseline for mixed riding conditions with moderate assist. If you're a heavier rider, tackle hills regularly, or like to ride in higher assist modes, push that reduction closer to 50 percent.

So a bike advertised at 60 miles becomes a more honest 30 to 42 miles for most riders in typical conditions. That's still a useful commuting range for most people, but it's a very different number than the one on the box.

If the brand offers a range calculator that accounts for rider weight, terrain, and assist level, use it. It's far more reliable than doing mental math off a single spec sheet figure. And if you can, test ride the exact model you're considering on terrain similar to your actual commute or trail. Nothing beats real data from your own legs.

## Battery Capacity Benchmarks: What Counts as Long Range in 2026

Battery sizes have crept up steadily over the past few years, and what counted as impressive in 2022 is now pretty average.

As of 2026, here's a rough guide to where things stand:

- **Under 400Wh**: entry-level range, typically 20 to 35 real-world miles
- **400 to 600Wh**: the current mainstream sweet spot, usually 35 to 55 real-world miles
- **600 to 750Wh**: genuinely long range, often 50 to 75 real-world miles depending on conditions
- **750Wh and up, or dual-battery setups**: extended range territory, sometimes exceeding 80 to 100 real-world miles

Dual-battery bikes deserve a quick mention here. Some commuter and touring models now let you run two batteries in parallel, effectively doubling capacity (and, unfortunately, weight and price too). For riders who genuinely need triple-digit range, this is often a smarter move than chasing a single oversized battery pack.

## Tips to Extend Your Range on Any E-Bike

You don't need a bigger battery to get more miles out of your bike. A few habit changes go a long way.

Keep tires properly inflated. This alone can meaningfully cut rolling resistance and free up a surprising amount of range. Ride in a lower assist level whenever you don't need the extra push, and save the high-power modes for hills or headwinds rather than the whole ride.

Pedal actively instead of letting the motor do all the work. Even light pedaling input reduces how hard the motor has to work, which stretches your battery noticeably further. And in cold weather, bring the battery inside when you're not riding. Lithium batteries lose efficiency in the cold, and storing them at room temperature helps preserve both range and long-term battery health.

Smart route planning helps too. If you have a choice between a hilly path and a flatter one that adds a mile or two, the flatter route often uses less total energy despite the extra distance.

  </FAQItem>
  <FAQItem question="Why is my real range shorter than advertised?">

  </FAQItem>
  <FAQItem question="Can I add a second battery for more range?">
    Some e-bike models support dual-battery configurations that roughly double your range, though this adds weight and cost. Check whether your specific bike supports this before buying an extra battery pack.
