You're standing in a Costco warehouse, and there's a folding e-bike sitting between the seasonal patio furniture and a pallet of protein bars. It's $499.99. Down the aisle, someone's kid is trying to open a jar of almond butter the size of a paint can. And you're thinking: is this actually a good bike, or am I about to buy a glorified electric toy because it happened to be next to the rotisserie chickens?

Fair question. I've spent a good chunk of time pulling apart what Costco actually sells in the folding e-bike category and stacking it up against the online-only names people usually recommend, Lectric, Velotric, Ride1Up. Nobody's really done a clean, side-by-side version of this comparison, probably because it means digging through two very different retail worlds. So here it is.

Short answer up front: it depends on what kind of buyer you are. Longer answer below.

## What Costco actually offers right now in folding e-bikes

Costco's folding e-bike selection is smaller and more fluid than most people expect. It's not a fixed lineup like you'd get from a bike-specific brand. It rotates.

Right now the flagship folding option is the **Hiboy C1**, a 350W, 14-inch-wheel commuter bike that Hiboy started placing in Costco warehouses this year as part of a push into physical retail. It lists for $499.99 online (sometimes less in-store, sometimes with a temporary $100-off promo), has dual disc brakes, a max speed of 20 mph, and a stated rider weight limit of 230 lbs. It's a genuinely small, light bike built for short hops, not for serious commuting distance.

Costco has also carried the **Jetson Bolt Pro** and **Jetson Bolt Pro Max** off and on for years. These are cheaper, more toy-adjacent 350W bikes with 14-inch wheels, a max speed around 15.5 mph on the Max, and weight capacities in the 220 lb range. They're fine for a kid or a light adult puttering around a driveway. They are not commuter bikes in any real sense.

Then there's the **Jetson OTG Elite**, which technically folds down (fold handlebars, fold-in pedals) but rides more like a kids' scooter-bike hybrid with 16-inch tires and a 20-mile range.

Here's the thing that trips people up: Costco's e-bike selection online often shows just one or two folding models "in stock" at a time, and availability swings by region and season. What's sitting in your local warehouse in June might be gone by September. That inconsistency alone is worth knowing before you build a purchase decision around "the Costco bike." ## How Costco models compare on spec to direct-to-consumer brands

This is where the gap becomes obvious. Put the Hiboy C1 next to the current class leader from Lectric, the **XP 3.0** at $999, and you're not really comparing two versions of the same product. You're comparing two different categories that both happen to fold.

The Hiboy C1 runs a 350W motor, 14-inch wheels, and a 36V/7.8Ah removable battery good for a real-world range in the 15-20 mile ballpark. The Lectric XP 3.0 runs a 500W hub motor (1,000W peak), fat 20x3-inch tires, a 500Wh battery rated for 45-65 miles depending on assist level, hydraulic disc brakes, a 7-speed Shimano drivetrain, and an unlocked top speed of 28 mph. It also ships about 85% assembled, which matters more than people think until they're the ones assembling it.

Velotric's entry-level folder, the **Fold 1**, lands around $1,199-$1,300 and brings a 750W rear-hub motor, a 608Wh battery, front suspension, and hydraulic brakes. The step-up **Fold 1 Plus** runs $1,499 and adds SensorSwap tech (switch between torque and cadence sensing), a 3.5-inch color display, and Apple Find My integration for theft recovery, a feature that's basically nonexistent at Costco's price tier.

Ride1Up's **Portola** sits at roughly $995-$999 and matches a lot of what the Fold 1 Plus offers, 750W motor, front suspension, Class 3 capability, at a noticeably lower price, which is exactly why reviewers keep cross-shopping it against the Velotric.

So what does this actually mean in practice? If you're commuting more than a couple miles each way, climbing any real hills, or riding with cargo, the Costco folding bikes in the sub-$500 tier are underpowered for the job. They're built for short, flat, casual use. The direct-to-consumer bikes at $999 to $1,500 are built as actual transportation.

## Return policy and warranty differences

This is honestly where Costco pulls ahead, and it's not close.

Costco operates under its long-standing 100% satisfaction guarantee. In practice, that means bikes, along with most general merchandise, can be returned essentially any time, ridden or not, for a full refund. There are documented cases of members returning e-bikes two-plus years after purchase and getting refunded in full. Some product categories (electronics like laptops and TVs) get a tighter 90-day window, but bikes generally aren't treated that way. If you want a no-risk way to try an e-bike without committing, this is about as generous as retail returns get.

Direct-to-consumer brands can't match that, and they're not really trying to. Lectric's return window runs 14 days from delivery, with the bike expected back in resellable condition. Velotric and Ride1Up run similar short windows, typically two to four weeks, and you're usually on the hook for return shipping unless the bike arrived defective. None of them offer anything close to "ride it for a year and change your mind."

Where it flips is ongoing warranty support. Lectric, Velotric, and Ride1Up all offer one- or two-year warranties on frame, motor, and battery, backed by dedicated customer service teams that specialize in e-bikes and know their own products inside out. Costco's folding bikes route warranty claims back to the manufacturer, Hiboy, Jetson, whoever, and Costco's own involvement mostly ends at the point of sale. If your Jetson battery dies in month eight, you're dealing with Jetson support, not a Costco employee.

So: Costco wins on the "what if I hate it" scenario. Direct brands win on the "what if something breaks after the honeymoon period" scenario.

## Where Costco wins (price, no-hassle returns, in-store trial)

Let's give credit where it's due.

**Price of entry.** At $499.99 or less with a promo, the Hiboy C1 is genuinely one of the cheapest ways to own a folding e-bike with disc brakes and pedal assist. Nothing from Lectric, Velotric, or Ride1Up touches that price point new.

**The return policy is a real safety net.** For someone who's never ridden an e-bike and isn't sure they'll actually use it, being able to return it essentially risk-free removes a lot of the anxiety around a several-hundred-dollar impulse purchase.

**You can see it in person.** Online reviews are great until you realize your torso is shaped differently than the reviewer's. Walking up to a bike in a warehouse, throwing a leg over it, and getting a feel for the fold mechanism before you buy has real value, even if Costco itself doesn't allow actual test rides in most locations.

**No membership fee shock if you already shop there.** If you're already paying for a Costco membership, the bike essentially comes with no additional buy-in cost the way ordering direct sometimes does when you factor in shipping.

## Where direct brands win (spec quality, customer support, upgrade options)

**Component quality, full stop.** Hydraulic brakes versus mechanical, torque sensors versus basic cadence sensing, real batteries in the 500-750Wh range versus sub-300Wh packs. At the $999-$1,500 tier, Lectric, Velotric, and Ride1Up are simply building a more capable bike.

**Range and power that match real commuting needs.** A 15-20 mile range is fine for errands. It's not fine if you're riding 8 miles each way to work and don't want to sweat the battery gauge on the way home.

**Customer support built around e-bikes specifically.** These companies live and die by e-bike reputation. Their support lines know torque arms, controller resets, and battery BMS quirks. A general Costco return desk doesn't, and won't try to.

**Upgrade paths and accessory ecosystems.** Passenger kits, official racks, matched panniers, app-based ride tuning, this stuff exists because these brands treat the bike as a platform, not a one-off SKU. Costco's folding bikes are mostly sold as-is.

**Consistent availability.** If you like your Lectric XP 3.0 and want a second one, or a part replacement, it's still sitting on the website next year. Costco's rotating selection means the exact model you bought might not exist there again.

## Verdict by buyer type

**First-time e-bike buyer, unsure if you'll even like it:** Costco. The return policy alone justifies starting there. Worst case, you return it and you're out nothing but the time.

**Daily commuter riding more than 3-5 miles each way:** Direct-to-consumer, specifically something like the Lectric XP 3.0 or Ride1Up Portola. The range, power, and brake quality difference isn't marginal at commuting distances.

**Budget under $600, casual neighborhood riding only:** Costco's Hiboy C1 or a Jetson model does the job and won't feel like a waste if it ends up parked most of the time.

**Buyer who wants tech features (theft tracking, app tuning, torque sensing):** Velotric Fold 1 Plus. Nothing at Costco's price tier comes close to matching Apple Find My integration or SensorSwap.

**Heavier riders or anyone carrying cargo regularly:** Direct brands. Costco's folding bikes tend to cap out around 220-230 lbs rider weight, while several direct-to-consumer models rate higher payload capacity and sturdier frames.

**Someone who just wants the absolute lowest sticker price and doesn't care about long-term support:** Costco, no contest.

There isn't one universal best folding e-bike on the market here. There's a best one for your specific situation, and the honest move is figuring out which bucket you fall into before you swipe a card.
