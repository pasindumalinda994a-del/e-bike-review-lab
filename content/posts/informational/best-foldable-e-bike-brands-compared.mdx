So you've decided you want a folding e-bike. Good move, honestly. But now you're staring at a wall of options that range from $900 to nearly $5,000, and every brand claims theirs folds the smallest, rides the smoothest, and fits under a desk like magic.

Here's the thing. They can't all be right. And the truth is, the "best" fold depends entirely on what you're folding it for.

I've spent a good chunk of time digging through specs, reviews, and real owner feedback on the three brands that dominate this space: Brompton, Tern, and the newer wave of budget folders like Lectric, Velotric, and MiRiDER. Each one solves the folding problem in a completely different way, and that difference matters way more than most buying guides let on.

Let's get into it.

## Brompton: the compact fold benchmark

If you've ever seen someone fold a bike into what looks like a small suitcase on a train platform in London, that was probably a Brompton. This is the brand every other folding bike gets measured against, and for good reason.

The standard Brompton Electric folds down to just 22.2 x 23 x 10.6 inches, which is roughly the footprint of an overstuffed carry-on bag. That's thanks to a clever three-part frame and small 16-inch wheels, a setup most competitors don't bother copying because it's genuinely hard to engineer well.

The catch, and there's always a catch, is price. The Brompton Electric starts around $3,499 in the US. If you want the newer G Line, which swaps in bigger 20-inch wheels for a ride that feels more like an actual bicycle over rough pavement, that model launched at $4,950 and folds to a slightly larger 28.3 x 26.2 x 16 inches while weighing about 35 pounds.

One reviewer who spent three weeks on the G Line put it well: compared to Brompton's own compact P Line, the G Line rides noticeably better and feels more stable, but it's heavier at 42.9 pounds versus 37, and it folds into a bigger package. So even within Brompton's own lineup, you're trading fold size for ride quality. That trade-off doesn't go away just because you paid a premium.

On the non-electric side, Brompton's pricing ladder gives you a sense of where the brand sits overall. The entry-level A Line starts at £999, while the popular C Line moves up from there, and the flagship titanium and carbon models push past £2,000. Add the e-Motiq electric system and you're solidly in premium territory no matter which line you pick.

Is it worth it? If your daily reality involves squeezing onto a packed commuter train or fitting a bike under a cafe table without anyone tripping over it, honestly, yes. Nothing else folds this small this reliably. But if you're mostly riding trails or hauling groceries, you're paying a lot for compactness you might not need.

## Tern: the do-it-all commuter fold

Tern takes a completely different approach. Instead of chasing the smallest possible fold, Tern builds bikes that fold fast, stand upright on their own, and can still be rolled around like a suitcase when collapsed.

Tern's patented N-Fold system rotates the front wheel 180 degrees so it sits parallel to the rear wheel, and the whole process takes just three steps and about 30 seconds. That might sound slower than a Brompton's near-instant fold, but there's a real upside: because the steering column stays straight and sits on the outside of the folded package, you can actually push a folded Tern along the ground instead of carrying it. If you've ever lugged a 20-pound bike up a flight of subway stairs, you'll understand why that matters.

Take the Vektron Q9, one of Tern's most popular electric folders. It folds to 16.1 x 33.9 x 26.8 inches and weighs 48.3 pounds, with a maximum rider weight rating of 231.5 pounds. That's a much bigger folded footprint than a Brompton, but it also uses a proper Bosch mid-drive motor and hydraulic disc brakes, components you typically don't see until you're well into premium e-bike territory.

Tern also backs its bikes with genuinely strong support. The company offers a 10-year extended warranty on the frame and fork, plus 7 years of guaranteed availability on replacement parts like Bosch batteries. That's rare in an industry where brands come and go, and it's a big part of why Tern owners tend to stick around for the long haul.

Where Tern really separates itself is versatility. The lineup ranges from the budget-friendly Link series up through cargo-hauling models like the GSD and HSD, which can carry up to 440 pounds of passengers and cargo while still folding down for storage. So if "folding bike" for you also means "needs to carry a kid or a week of groceries," Tern has an answer Brompton simply doesn't.

The trade-off is obvious once you pick one up. These bikes are heavier and bulkier folded than a Brompton, and that 33-inch-tall folded package won't slide under a train seat the way a Brompton will. You're choosing capability and ride feel over ultimate compactness.

## Budget brands closing the gap (Lectric, Velotric, MiRiDER)

For years, "cheap folding e-bike" basically meant "compromised folding e-bike." That's changed a lot recently, and it's worth taking these brands seriously now instead of writing them off.

Lectric is probably the name you've seen most. The XP 3.0 starts at just $999 and comes with a 500-watt rear hub motor, hydraulic disc brakes, and a real-world range between 25 and 40 miles. The trade-off for that price is weight. At 64 pounds, it's noticeably heavier than a Brompton or even a Tern, which makes carrying it up stairs a real chore. But if you're mostly storing it in a car trunk or a closet rather than hand-carrying it onto transit, that weight matters a lot less.

What really stands out about the XP 3.0 is who it's built for. Its 330-pound payload capacity is generous for a folding e-bike, making it a genuinely solid option for heavier riders who often get overlooked by more compact, delicate designs. A lot of premium folders quietly cap out around 220 to 250 pounds. Lectric doesn't.

Velotric has come at the budget space from a slightly different angle, focusing on components that punch above their price point. The Discover 1 Plus, priced at $1,299, includes a torque sensor instead of the cheaper cadence sensor most budget bikes rely on, which makes the pedal assist feel far more natural. Its dedicated folder, the Fold 1, comes in under $1,000 and is built to be accessible for a wide range of riders, though it's not the lightest thing to haul around and runs a bit small for anyone over six feet tall.

Then there's MiRiDER, a British brand that's become a favorite for riders who specifically want a small, light fold without Brompton money. The MiRiDER One weighs about 17.3 kg (38 pounds) and folds to roughly 830 x 430 x 680 mm. That's genuinely competitive with premium folders. What surprised me most, though, is the weight rating. The MiRiDER One has a maximum rider weight of 120 kg (265 pounds), which is notably higher than many pricier folding e-bikes on the market.

The honest takeaway here: budget doesn't mean bad anymore. It means different priorities. You're usually trading a slightly bulkier or heavier fold, and sometimes a shorter warranty, for a price that's a third or less of what Brompton or Tern charge. ## Which fold size actually fits public transit rules

This is the part most comparison articles skip entirely, and it's arguably the most useful section if you're actually planning to commute with one of these bikes.

Different transit systems have wildly different rules, and your dream bike might be a headache in practice depending on where you live.

**Amtrak** is fairly specific. Folding bicycles under 34 x 15 x 48 inches are allowed onboard in place of a checked bag, though only certain cars can accommodate them and larger folders may need to be checked instead. Nearly every bike in this article, even the bulkier Tern Vektron, technically fits that box. Electric bikes specifically need to weigh under 50 pounds to qualify, which rules out the Lectric XP 3.0 and Velotric options and leaves Brompton and MiRiDER as your realistic picks for Amtrak trips.

**NYC's MTA** is more relaxed about size and stricter about behavior. Folding bikes are allowed almost everywhere in the system as long as they're actually folded, though they're not permitted on express buses, and commuter rail lines like the LIRR and Metro-North restrict standard bikes during rush hour in the peak direction. Since folding bikes are generally exempt from those rush-hour restrictions, this is really a "does it actually fold small enough to not annoy other riders" situation rather than a hard rule you'll get stopped for.

The general pattern across most US transit systems: folding bikes get treated like carry-on luggage, not like bicycles, as long as they're genuinely folded and not blocking doors or aisles. Weight and battery certification matter more than exact dimensions in most cases, which is actually good news if you're eyeing one of the bulkier Tern or Velotric models for local trains and buses rather than intercity Amtrak trips.

If your commute involves Amtrak or any service with a hard size cap, measure twice before you buy. If it's mostly subway, bus, or light rail, you have a lot more flexibility than the marketing photos suggest.

## Our recommendation by rider type

There's no single "best" here, so let's break it down by who you actually are.

**If you commute daily by train and space is tight:** Go Brompton. The fold size genuinely isn't matched by anyone else, and if you're doing this five days a week for years, the price stops looking as steep once you divide it out.

**If you want one bike that handles commuting, errands, and maybe hauling a kid around:** Tern. The upright, roll-along fold and the cargo-capable models in the lineup make it the most flexible brand here, and that 10-year warranty is hard to argue with.

**If you're a heavier rider who's been overlooked by "compact" bike weight limits:** Lectric XP 3.0. That 330-pound payload capacity is one of the most generous in this entire category, and you're not paying a premium for it.

**If you want the lightest possible fold without Brompton pricing:** MiRiDER One. It's the closest thing to a Brompton alternative that doesn't require a four-figure jump in price.

**If this is your first e-bike and you're not sure how much you'll actually use it:** Velotric Fold 1 or Lectric XP 3.0. Both sit right around $999, and honestly, that's a smart place to start before you commit to a pricier brand.

At the end of the day, the best fold is the one that fits your actual routine, not the one with the flashiest folded dimensions on a spec sheet.
