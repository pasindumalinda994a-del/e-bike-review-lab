If you've spent any time in off-road motorcycle circles, you already know the name Graham Jarvis. If you haven't, here's the short version: the man is arguably the most technically gifted extreme enduro rider alive. And in 2026, he's putting his name on something a lot of longtime fans didn't see coming. An electric bike.

Not an electric bicycle. A full electric hard enduro motorcycle, built for the same brutal terrain that made him famous. Let's get into what's actually going on here, because there's a fair amount of confusion floating around about what Jarvis rides and why.

## Who Is Graham Jarvis, and What Does He Actually Ride?

Graham Jarvis was born in Ripon, North Yorkshire, back in 1975, and he's spent basically his whole life on two wheels. His trophy case is absurd. Four Scottish Six Days Trial wins. Nine Scott Trial wins, more than anyone in the event's history. Five British Trials championships. Five British Enduro titles. Then there's the extreme enduro side of his career: seven Red Bull Romaniacs wins, five Erzberg Rodeo Hare Scramble titles, six Red Bull Sea to Sky wins, and multiple Hells Gate and Roof of Africa victories.

Since 2018, Jarvis has been a factory rider for Husqvarna Motorcycles, competing on a Husqvarna TE 300 built specifically for hard enduro's slow, technical, rock-crawling style of racing. That's his day job bike, and it's still a gas-powered machine.

So here's the thing people mix up. Jarvis hasn't switched to riding electric competitively. What he's done is start building electric bikes on the side, for a different purpose entirely.

## Electric vs. Gas-Powered Trials Bikes in Extreme Enduro

Hard enduro is a weird sport if you've never watched it. Picture a motorcycle crawling up a near-vertical rock face at walking speed, the rider dabbing a foot down every few seconds to keep balance, engine barely above idle. It's less about horsepower and more about control, and that's exactly why electric power has started creeping into the conversation.

Electric motors deliver instant, smooth torque with no clutch to feather and no gears to hunt through. In theory, that's perfect for the kind of millimeter-precise throttle control hard enduro demands. Riders don't need to worry about stalling on a technical climb, and the lack of engine vibration and noise makes it easier to focus purely on balance.

The catch has always been weight and battery life. Early electric off-road bikes tended to be heavier than their gas equivalents, and one dead battery meant the ride was over. That's the gap Jarvis and his partners are trying to close.

## The Real Story: JARV-E, Graham Jarvis's Electric Hard Enduro Venture

In 2026, Jarvis co-founded JARV-E Moto with Dave Freidinger, a chassis fabricator and race-bike engineer who's spent close to twenty years building frames and geometry setups across motocross, hard enduro, and Quad-MX. Freidinger had already built an electric enduro prototype called EDURO several years earlier. When Jarvis crossed paths with that bike at a race event, the two decided to team up and build something more serious together.

The result is the JARV-E, an electric hard enduro motorcycle built in Austria and designed from scratch around trials and hard enduro geometry rather than adapted from a motocross platform. A few of the numbers stand out. The bike weighs in at roughly 95 kilograms ready to ride, which the company says puts it around 25 kilograms lighter than a Stark Varg, one of the better-known electric off-road bikes currently on the market. Power output sits at about 50 kilowatts, which works out to roughly 68 horsepower.

Battery swaps take about 30 seconds, and the 5.6 kWh pack can go from empty to full in around an hour and a half on a 3.4 kW charger. Riders get up to five hours of use in the most conservative Super Eco mode, with power delivery, regenerative braking, and ergonomics all adjustable through an app.

JARV-E is launching with a limited Graham Jarvis Signature RAW edition, available for preorder with a small deposit, ahead of broader production. Given how quickly plans like this can shift, it's worth checking the JARV-E site directly for the latest word on pricing and delivery timing before assuming anything is locked in.

## Why These Machines Are Built Differently From Consumer E-Bikes

This is the part that trips people up. A bike like the JARV-E has almost nothing in common with the electric bikes most people are shopping for, the kind built for commuting or weekend trail riding. It's not pedal-assisted. There's no throttle limiter capping it at a bicycle-legal speed. It's a full motorcycle, built to survive rock gardens, root climbs, and repeated hard landings that would destroy a consumer e-bike frame in an afternoon.

The chassis geometry, suspension travel, and weight distribution are all tuned around a professional rider standing on the pegs and shifting body weight constantly, not someone sitting comfortably on a saddle. Even the "adjustability" JARV-E talks about, tuning regen strength or throttle response through an app, exists to fine-tune competition performance, not to make the ride gentler for a casual owner.

None of this is registered or intended for street use. It's built for closed courses and off-road terrain, the same world Jarvis has raced in his entire career.

## What Everyday Riders Can (and Can't) Learn From Race-Spec Bikes

So does any of this matter if you're just looking at electric bikes for your commute or weekend trail rides? A little, actually.

The instant, smooth torque delivery that makes electric motors so useful for technical crawling is the same quality that makes a good electric mountain bike or commuter e-bike feel so different from a traditional bicycle. You get power the moment you need it, without hunting for the right gear. Battery swap systems, still rare on consumer e-bikes, are also starting to trickle down from race and delivery applications into everyday models, and that's worth watching if range anxiety is your main hesitation.

What doesn't translate is the price, the maintenance demands, or the sheer durability overkill. A race-spec hard enduro machine is solving problems most riders will never actually have.
